## 1. Roman siege works at Alesia (BG VII.72-73)

### 1a. Latin text, verbatim (from LAT-LIB)

Section 72:
- "Fossam pedum viginti directis lateribus duxit, ut eius fossae solum tantundem pateret quantum summae fossae labra distarent." (A trench 20 feet wide, with straight sides, so that the floor was as wide as the top edge.)
- "Reliquas omnes munitiones ab ea fossa pedes quadringentos reduxit," (He set back all the other works 400 **feet** from that trench.)
- "Hoc intermisso spatio duas fossas quindecim pedes latas, eadem altitudine perduxit," (In that gap he dug two trenches, each 15 feet wide, of equal depth.)
- "Post eas aggerem ac vallum duodecim pedum exstruxit." (Behind them he built an earthwork and palisade 12 feet high.)
- "et turres toto opere circumdedit, quae pedes LXXX inter se distarent." (He put towers all round the works, 80 feet apart.)

Section 73:
- "perpetuae fossae quinos pedes altae ducebantur." (Continuous trenches, 5 feet deep, were dug. These hold the **cippi**.)
- "Ante quos obliquis ordinibus in quincuncem dispositis scrobes tres in altitudinem pedes fodiebantur" (In front of these, in slanting rows arranged in a quincunx, pits 3 feet deep were dug. These are the **lilia**.)
- "ita ut non amplius digitis quattuor ex terra eminerent;" (...so that they stood no more than four fingers above the ground.)
- "Huius generis octoni ordines ducti ternos inter se pedes distabant." (Eight rows of this kind were dug, 3 feet apart.)
- "Ante haec taleae pedem longae ferreis hamis infixis totae in terram infodiebantur" (In front of these, logs a foot long with iron hooks fixed in them were buried entirely in the ground. These are the **stimuli**.)

Note on the Latin: the "400" is **feet** (pedes), not paces. Some English summaries say paces. Use feet.

Other summary-level description (not Latin-verified): WIKI-EN-LOANWORDS and LIVIUS describe the order from outside in: stimuli, lilia, cippi, two trenches, palisade with towers. LIVIUS says the field of cippi was "tombstones" (artificial underwood). The Latin order from the Latin text itself is: main trench, then 400 feet back, two 15-foot trenches, rampart and palisade, towers. Game designers should use the Latin order for the wall and the trap order from LIVIUS for the outer field.

### 1c. Archaeology versus Caesar (1991-1997 excavations)

- Excavations were run from 1991 to 1997 by a Franco-German team led by Michel Reddé and Siegmar von Schnurbein. Results were published in 2001. Source: WIKI-FR-ALESIA; UNIVERSALIS; REDDE-2001.
- WIKI-FR-ALESIA (summarising the excavations) says the finds confirmed the topography and identification of the site and included material from the late La Tène and end of the Republic.
- WIKI-FR-ALESIA lists the main ditch as about 4.50 m wide and of equal depth. It says it filled with water within an hour of being dug. Caesar's 20 feet is about 5.9 m. **[VERIFY: this is a secondary summary; check REDDE-2001 for the measured width.]**
- WIKI-FR-ALESIA gives the excavated rampart as about 3.50 m high, with a palisade (pluteus) on top. The same page says the tower spacing observed in the field can vary a lot from Caesar's 80 feet.
- UNIVERSALIS (search snippet): the wall had towers every 15 to 17 metres, with a system of ditches and traps in front. Traps: conical holes (lilia), small ditches filled with interlaced branches (cippi), or metal points stuck in the ground (stimuli).
- Conflicting view: a museum notice from the 1994 Vercingetorix exhibition (RMN) says the cippi had disappeared and the lilia had not been found. It also places the rampart behind a third ditch that Caesar does not mention. This is an earlier reading, reported in a search snippet only. **[VERIFY]**
- WIKI-EN-ALESIA: "Archeological evidence suggests the lines were not continuous as Caesar claims," and "modern archeology suggests that Caesar's preparations were not as complete as he describes."
- WIKI-FR-ALESIA says the excavations confirmed the 19th-century identification at Alise-Sainte-Reine, which is widely accepted.
- I did not find a source that uses "trous de loup" for the Alesia traps. Use "lilia" and "cippes" as Caesar does.

## 2. Order of events in 52 BC

Sources used: WIKI-EN-GW (navbox and Avaricum/Gergovia text), LIVIUS, WIKI-FR-ALESIA, THAYER (ch. 3).

Approximate order (no exact days are reliable except for Alesia):

1. Early 52 BC (winter): Carnutes massacre Roman traders at **Cenabum** (Orléans) and kill the Roman grain officer Gaius Fufius Cita. THAYER (BG VII.3). Caesar is in Italy at the start of the year and rushes north. Source for the date: THAYER/Caesar; the date is "early in the year" in Caesar's text. **[VERIFY exact dating]**
2. Winter 52 BC: Vercingetorix is chosen leader at a Gallic assembly at **Bibracte** (Mont Beuvray). WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
3. Late winter: Caesar takes oppida for food and forces Vercingetorix to lift his siege of Gorgobina. WIKI-EN-GW (summary of Caesar's text).
4. Winter/spring: **Siege of Avaricum (Bourges)**. Vercingetorix had ordered the Gauls to burn their towns, but the Bituriges begged to keep Avaricum. The Romans built a camp in about 25 days and took the town during a rainstorm. Caesar's figure: 40,000 killed (WIKI-EN-GW, citing Caesar). WIKI-EN-GW notes historians doubt contemporary numbers.
5. Spring 52 BC: **Siege of Gergovia** (Arverni, near Clermont-Ferrand). The Roman assault failed. Caesar's figure: 700 Romans killed, including 46 centurions (WIKI-EN-GW, citing Caesar). Caesar's own losses are likely understated (WIKI-EN-ALESIA).
6. Summer 52 BC: **Battle of the Vingeanne** (WIKI-EN-GW lists it at 52 BC, with Caesar defeating Vercingetorix's cavalry). **[VERIFY: LIVIUS places the cavalry battle at Les Laumes near Alesia, and WIKI-FR-ALESIA places it during the Roman retreat in Sequani territory. These are different accounts; choose one for the game and say so.]**
7. July to September 52 BC: **Siege of Alesia**. Caesar surrounds the town with two lines of works. The relief army arrives late. WIKI-FR-ALESIA gives July to September, with the relief army arriving "perhaps at the end of September." WIKI-EN-GW gives "September 52 BC."
8. The main battle (fourth day of the relief attack, per LIVIUS) fails. Vercingetorix surrenders the next day. WIKI-FR-ALESIA says the exact date is not given.

## 4. Roman army versus Gallic warriors

### Roman equipment

- **Pilum** (heavy javelin): about 2 m long overall. The iron shank is about 7 mm in diameter and 600 mm long. Weight 1 to 2.5 kg. Effective range 15 to 20 m, recent experiments suggest up to 33 m. Source: WIKI-EN-PILUM. The page gives no source for the weight, and calls it "citation needed."
- Bending of the pilum: WIKI-EN-PILUM says the bending was not clearly intended. Views differ between Secci (2012), who says it was meant to break, and Bishop & Coulston (2009), who say bending was unintended but useful. **[VERIFY: do not present "pilum bends so it cannot be thrown back" as settled fact.]**
- **Gladius** (short stabbing sword): legionaries threw the pilum just before closing in with the gladius. WIKI-EN-PILUM (Slavik 2017; Cowan 2003).
- **Scutum** (shield): no fetched source gives its dimensions. [general knowledge: large curved rectangular shield.]

### Gallic equipment and tactics

- **Carnyx**: a copper-alloy war horn, sometimes taller than a man. Used to rally troops and frighten the enemy. Evidence: Delphi 279 BC, Caesar's campaigns, Tintignac (seven carnyces found). Source: a CAMWS/search snippet, not fetched in full. [VERIFY with the CAMWS abstract: https://camws.org/sites/default/files/meeting2018/abstracts/411.Carnyx.pdf]
- **Torc** (neck ring): worn by elite warriors (the gaesatae, per the search snippet). Source: search snippet, not fetched in full.
- **Long sword**: Celtic warriors mostly had long, blunt-ended swords that were good for slashing in open ground but awkward in crowded close fighting. Source: same search snippet. Caesar's account is also used by WIKI-EN-ALESIA.
- Celtic battles often turned on single combat, where a champion challenged the enemy before the lines met. Source: search snippet.
- Source caveat: almost all our written evidence is Greek or Roman. Accounts of warriors fighting naked are contested.
- **Cavalry**: Caesar's Gallic cavalry at Alesia is given as 8,000 (WIKI-FR-ALESIA, Caesar's figure). Roman cavalry is not given in the fetched text.

[VERIFY: the pilum and gladius facts are from WIKI-EN-PILUM. The carnyx, torc and sword facts are only from search snippets, not from full pages.]

## 5. Gallic coins, oppida, murus gallicus

### Coins

- Gallic staters (gold or electrum) almost all come from one prototype: the gold stater of Philip II of Macedon, brought back by Gallic mercenaries. WIKI-FR-STATERE.
- The first tribes to make faithful copies "seem to be" the Arverni (Auvergne) and Helvetii (Switzerland). WIKI-FR-STATERE.
- Over time the Apollo head on the front became more abstract and the chariot turned into a single horse with a flying charioteer. WIKI-FR-STATERE.
- **Vercingetorix coin**: a rare gold stater of the Arverni, in the name of Vercingetorix. Head of curls on one side; galloping horse under a crescent moon over an amphora on the other. Diameter 1.9 cm. Dated 52 BC. Probably from the Pionsat treasure (found 1852, Puy-de-Dôme). MBA-LYON.
- Search snippets disagree on where the legend VERCINGETORIXS sits (one side or the other). MBA-LYON does not give the legend. **[VERIFY: check a catalogue, such as the Lyon museum record, before showing it in the game.]**
- A dealer source says fewer than 30 coins in the name of Vercingetorix are known. Search snippet, not verified. Weight given as 7.44 g in one snippet (not in MBA-LYON).

### Oppida and Bibracte

- Bibracte: the capital of the Aedui, on Mont Beuvray (Saône-et-Loire and Nièvre). WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- Dates: second to first century BC. Founded toward the end of the second century BC. Abandoned after Augustodunum (Autun) was founded around 15 BC. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- Area: about 135 hectares inside the inner rampart. The first outer rampart enclosed about 200 hectares. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- Inner rampart: a murus gallicus about 5 km long. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- 52 BC: a Gallic assembly at Bibracte gave Vercingetorix supreme command. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- Excavations: Bulliot 1867-1895, Déchelette until 1907, resumed 1984, European archaeological centre opened 1995. As of 2024 about 12 hectares partly excavated. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE.
- Museum: the "musée de Bibracte," opened 1996. WIKI-FR-BIBRACTE. (The name "MuséoParc Bibracte" does not appear on the page.)

### Murus gallicus (construction)

- Timber beams are laid across the line of the wall with gaps of about 2 feet. The beams are linked, and covered with a lot of earth.
- The outer face is built of large stones set into the gaps. A second layer of beams sits above the first, offset so no beam touches the one below. Stones separate the beams.
- Height about 4 m. Source: WIKI-FR-MURUS. No width given.
- Caesar's reasoning (BG VII.23, as quoted in WIKI-FR-MURUS): stone protects against fire, and timber protects against the battering ram. A footnote notes that timber also adds fuel to a fire, and ruins in France and Scotland show vitrified stone.
- The first excavated murus gallicus was at Murcens (Lot) in 1867. WIKI-FR-MURUS. Alesia appears only in the bibliography of that page. WIKI-FR-MURUS does not say that murus gallicus was found at Alesia.

## 7. What Caesar exaggerated versus what the sources say

### Numbers (from WIKI-EN-ALESIA, WIKI-FR-ALESIA, WIKI-EN-GW)

| Item | Caesar's figure | Modern view |
|---|---|---|
| Gauls inside Alesia | 80,000 (WIKI-FR-ALESIA) | Modern estimates 20,000 to 80,000 (WIKI-EN-ALESIA) |
| Gallic relief army | About 250,000 infantry plus 8,000 cavalry (WIKI-FR-ALESIA); ancient sources 248,000 to 300,000 (WIKI-EN-ALESIA) | 50,000 to 100,000 (WIKI-EN-ALESIA). WIKI-EN-ALESIA: "probably an exaggeration to enhance his victory" |
| Total Gauls (ancient) | 328,000 (Caesar, BG VII.71 and 76 as cited); Plutarch 470,000; Strabo 400,000 | Under 70,000 to 180,000 total (WIKI-EN-ALESIA) |
| Gallic casualties | 290,000 (WIKI-EN-ALESIA: "Caesar, exaggerated") | Modern estimate about 250,000 killed and 40,000 captured (WIKI-EN-ALESIA) |
| Roman forces | No single figure in WIKI-EN-ALESIA from Caesar; WIKI-FR-ALESIA says 10 to 12 legions | About 60,000 to 75,000 Romans and allies (WIKI-EN-ALESIA); 50,000 to 72,000 Romans plus 10,000 Germans (WIKI-FR-ALESIA) |
| Avaricum killed | 40,000 (WIKI-EN-GW) | Historians doubt contemporary numbers (David Henige, cited in a search snippet of WIKI-EN-GW) |
| Gergovia Roman losses | 700 killed including 46 centurions (WIKI-EN-GW) | Caesar's own losses likely understated (WIKI-EN-ALESIA) |
| Earthwork length | Outer line about 14 miles, inner about 10 miles (WIKI-FR-ALESIA) | About 15 km circuit (LIVIUS); about 40 km of works (WIKI-FR-ALESIA, from modern estimate) |

Notes:
- The "328,000" total is from Caesar's text as given in WIKI-EN-ALESIA. The page also reports "328,000 to 470,000" as ancient totals. Keep the range.
- The Caesar figures above come from secondary summaries of BG VII.71 and 76. The Latin for those chapters was not fetched. **[VERIFY against the Latin before quoting exact numbers in the game.]**

### Archaeology versus Caesar's works

- Caesar says the Roman works had a main trench 20 feet wide. Archaeology gives about 4.5 m (about 15 feet) for the excavated ditch (WIKI-FR-ALESIA). **[VERIFY]**
- Caesar says the towers were 80 feet apart. The excavated spacing is said to vary a lot (WIKI-FR-ALESIA), and UNIVERSALIS gives 15 to 17 metres.
- Caesar's lines were presented as continuous. Archaeology suggests they were not (WIKI-EN-ALESIA).
- Archaeology has traces of cippi and lilia in some accounts (UNIVERSALIS), but a 1994 museum notice says the cippi had disappeared and the lilia were not found. **[VERIFY]**
- Caesar's preparations were not as complete as he describes (WIKI-EN-ALESIA).
