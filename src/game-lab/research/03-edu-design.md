# Educational history games: design research for the Alésia site

Scope: short (1–3 min) browser mini-games for 12–16 year olds. Two modes: whole-class voting on a projector, and solo play on phones. Research done with WebSearch plus page fetches on 2026-11-16. Where a claim rests on one blog or a vendor, it is flagged.

## 1. Evidence overview

- **Games do help learning on average, but design matters more than medium.** Clark et al. (K–16 digital games meta-analysis) found design features moderated outcomes beyond the medium itself. Source: https://www.gamesandlearning.org (Clark et al. meta-analysis summary, reachable via search results on gamesandlearning.org). A nine-study serious-games meta-analysis reported d = 0.637 (95% CI [0.483, 0.791]). Source: search result summary, https://doaj.org/article/670d7a53fdad46cbb7c37657af4f5dd4 (Yu Zhonggen 2019 review, decade of serious-games literature).
- **No strong meta-analysis found specifically for history games.** Treat history-specific claims below as practitioner evidence.
- **Vendor evidence is inflated.** Kahoot's "0.72 SD" and "70% of studies" figures come from pages Kahoot publishes or commissions (https://kahoot.com/blog/2020/07/01/dozens-of-studies-show-learning-benefits-of-kahoot/, https://kahoot.com/blog/2024/06/06/kahoot-gold-standard-evidence-based-learning/). Blooket evidence is anecdotal (https://sites.google.com/view/gbl-edcp544/blooket/implementation-experiment, a single Coquitlam secondary school, no score comparison).
- **Engagement gains are more consistent than durable learning gains.** The Kahoot/Blooket literature mostly shows motivation and engagement; learning effects depend on whether the game is tied to the lesson objective.
- **A cautionary result on source criticism.** A Swedish thesis on teaching source criticism through an educational computer game found no significant knowledge gain after play. Source: search summary, see Swedish-thesis entry in the Gaming the Past / search results, not verified in full. Implication: the game must be paired with explicit source-analysis tasks, not expected to teach it by itself.

## 2. Classroom fit for 12–16 year olds

### Whole-class projector voting
- Use one projected game, one vote per round, and a discussion step after each vote. The Edutopia Rome II lesson did exactly this: a volunteer played the projected game while peers gave feedback on each turn, and "the turn-based design gives time for deliberate decisions." Source: https://www.edutopia.org/article/exploring-ancient-history-video-games
- Turn-based or round-based mechanics suit projector play. Real-time reflexes do not, because the class can only watch one screen.
- Keep the vote visible on the board before reveal, so the class commits first and then debates.

### Session length
- Board-game guidance for a class period: 15–20 min of play, 5–8 min of explanation, about 50 minutes total with discussion. Source: https://cha-shc.ca/teachers-learning-bl/games-and-the-history-classroom/ (practitioner advice for custom games).
- For Alésia mini-games, a 2-minute game plus a 5-minute debrief fits a lesson slot. Do not exceed three games in one lesson without a break.

### Competition vs cooperation
- Kahoot/Blooket-style competition gives speed bonuses. Speed rewards quick guessing, not reasoning, which works against historical thinking. Prefer scoring on quality of choice or on "best outcome for the Gauls" style goals, or hide speed from the leaderboard.
- For the projector mode, a collective goal (the class decides together) avoids a single winner and avoids the "shouting the answer" effect.
- For phone solo mode, a personal best and a "try another ending" loop works better than a leaderboard.

### Kahoot/Blooket-style vs narrative games
- Quiz-style rounds are fine for vocabulary and dates (short recall). They are the weakest format for causation and perspective, which is what the site needs.
- Narrative or decision games fit "what would you have done" and counterfactuals. Keep the quiz-style items as a short warm-up only.

## 3. Teaching historical thinking through games

### Source criticism ("according to Caesar…")
- Treat the game as a primary source, like a cartoon or photograph. Analyse its assumptions, rules and what it makes easy or hard. Source: https://www.edutopia.org/article/exploring-ancient-history-video-games and https://cha-shc.ca/teachers-learning-bl/games-and-the-history-classroom/
- Build each mini-game around a visible source. Example: after playing a Caesar choice, show a short excerpt from Gallic Wars and ask "what does Caesar leave out?"
- Argument from silence works well: what does the source not mention (for example, Caesar's debts in the Rome II debrief)?

### Perspective-taking
- Let students play as the Gauls inside Alésia, or as a Roman officer, and then compare with Caesar's text. Ask "whose story is this?" before and after.
- Show that players' choices diverge from the record. The Rome II lesson asked "why did your gameplay differ from the historical record?" Source: Edutopia link above.

### Counterfactuals
- Use "what if?" as an explicit mode. The Rome II lesson framed Caesar losing to the Helvetii as an alternative history and asked students to explain how and why outcomes diverge. Source: Edutopia link above.
- Require the student to name the historical factor that changed the outcome (supply, terrain, allies). This keeps counterfactuals tied to causation.

### Causation
- Give players levers that map to causes historians cite: supply, relief army timing, Gallic cooperation, Roman fortifications. Ask after each round which lever mattered.
- Caveat from the Europa Universalis IV study: games can misrepresent history through abstraction. Always pair a game with a short "what the game simplified" card.

## 4. Sensitive history: Alésia specifics

Sources:
- Mission US: Flight to Freedom critique (choice-based rewards for cruelty, badges for compliance): search summary, see https://msmagazine.com/?p=273160 and https://www.minnpost.com/learning-curve/2015/02/minneapolis-school-officials-respond-controversy-over-slavery-game/
- "Slave Tetris" critique: search summary, see https://news.yahoo.com/teachers-gamers-agree-slave-tetris-isnt-educate-kids-205809452.html
- Cult of Pedagogy episode 125 (guest Hasan Kwame Jeffries argues teachers should avoid simulations of slavery): https://cultofpedagogy.libsyn.com/webpage/125-think-twice-before-doing-another-historical-simulation
- Reacting Consortium "Addressing Discomfort" guidance (pre-game discussion): https://www.reactingconsortium.org/resources/Documents/General/RGR-AddressingDiscomfort.pdf

Design rules drawn from these:
1. **Do not gamify suffering.** No points, badges or "score" for starving civilians or for enslaving captives. Critics specifically named badges for cruelty.
2. **Make the cost visible without making it a mechanic.** Show the consequence as a text card and a source quote, not as a bar that fills up or a number that drops.
3. **Give a cause, not a reward.** For Alésia's siege, starvation inside the walls is a result of the Roman encirclement. Show it as a systemic outcome with a historical quote, not as a player choice that wins or loses.
4. **Avoid "win" framing for the post-surrender enslavement.** Caesar's account (Gallic War, book 7) records captives being distributed as booty. The game should present this as a historical fact with a source, with a discussion prompt, and without an interactive decision that the player can "optimise".
5. **Name the people.** Show Vercingetorix's surrender and the civilians as people with names or roles where sources allow.
6. **Pre-game framing.** A one-line teacher note before play, and a debrief question after, as the Reacting Consortium recommends.
7. **Give an exit.** Provide a "skip this scene" or "read instead" option for students who find a scene upsetting.
8. **Keep the tone plain.** Avoid dramatic music, gore or graphic images. Use the sources' own register.

## 5. Mobile and touch UX for very short games

Sources found are general. Specific haptics guidance was not found in search.
- Keep onboarding short and do not front-load text. Source: https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2014/04/rethinking-mobile-tutorials-which-patterns-really-work/
- Teach in context, not as exposition. Source: https://www.blog.udonis.co/mobile-marketing/mobile-games/mobile-game-tutorial
- Introduce one mechanic, repeat it with variation, then raise challenge. Source: https://nerdyteachers.com/PICO-8/Game_Design/105
- Apple HIG: keep splash and onboarding brief. Source: https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/onboarding.md
- Avoid too many concurrent touch mechanics. Source: https://mobilefreetoplay.com/bible/user-experience-design-uiux-onboarding/

Practical rules for us (my synthesis, not directly sourced):
- One-screen onboarding: a single sentence and one icon showing the tap or drag. Maximum one line of text per mini-game.
- Single-gesture input: one tap or one drag per decision. No multi-finger gestures.
- Haptics: use only as confirmation of a choice (a short tap). Do not use haptics for consequences like "starving," and check whether the target device supports vibration before relying on it. iOS Safari does not expose the Vibration API, so haptics should be optional.
- Replayability: one "try another choice" button at the end of each game, with a different source-based question, not a higher score.
- Portrait-first layout, large tap targets (at least 44 px), no reliance on hover.
- Projector mode: the same game must work on a laptop at 1080p with the phone-sized layout centred, and with text large enough to read from the back of a classroom.

## 6. Pitfalls to avoid

- **Chocolate-covered broccoli.** Points and badges bolted onto a lesson. Amy Bruckman's 1999 paper "Can Educational Be Fun?" and Brenda Laurel's "Utopian Entrepreneur" describe the split between game and learning. Sources: search summary, https://www.artsjournal.org/?p=119042 and https://fatalinput.blogspot.com/2017/02/chocolate-covered-broccoli-critique-on.html. Rule: the historical question must be the game mechanic, not a quiz after it.
- **Quiz disguised as a game.** If every round is "pick the right date", students will guess. Use decisions with trade-offs.
- **Kids see through educational games.** Ian Harper (quoted in an industry piece): "Kids can see through games that are made for educational purposes." Rule: make the mini-games good enough to play for their own sake.
- **Technology failure.** The CHA page warns that technology can fail. Rule: keep a paper or slide fallback for each mini-game.
- **Overlong games.** Civilization-style games eat class time. Keep to 2–3 minutes.
- **Building too much.** Custom board games take time to make and playtest. Start with the topic you already find hardest to teach.
- **Accuracy drift.** Games simplify. Always pair with a "what was simplified" card and the primary source.

## 7. Concrete rules for the site

1. Every mini-game must ask a historical question (cause, perspective, source, counterfactual), not a recall question.
2. Each game has one visible primary source attached, with a "according to…" prompt.
3. Projector mode: round-based, one vote per round, discussion after each reveal.
4. Phone mode: one-tap decisions, one-line onboarding, a "try another choice" loop.
5. No speed scoring; no leaderboard for historical content.
6. Sensitive scenes (starvation at Alésia, post-surrender captives): no points, no badges, sourced text, a skip option, and a teacher pre-game note.
7. Session length: 2–3 min per game, 5–8 min debrief, max three games per lesson.
8. Each game ends with a counterfactual or causation question that students answer aloud or in writing.
9. Keep a non-digital fallback for each game.
10. Test with a real class of 12–16 year olds before release.
